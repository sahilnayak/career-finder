# "Why do you want to work here?"

Also asked as: why [Company], why this role, why us, what draws you to us, why are you
interested in this position.

## What the question is actually testing

The interviewer is not collecting compliments. They are asking for a reason to want you more
than they did a minute ago. Answers like "you have a great culture", "you changed the world",
or "everyone I know uses your product" are not wrong, they are just neutral: they give the
interviewer nothing to hire on, and every other candidate says them.

A good answer does two jobs at once. It answers *why here* **and** it sells the candidate.

## Structure — the two-beat framework

Total answer: **"A and B problems are interesting to me, for I and J reasons."** One pair
per beat. Never two products in Beat 2.

### Beat 1 — the opener

> "I'm interested in working for **[Company]** because I enjoy **[problem A]**, and **[personal reason I]**."

One sentence. Then stop. The point is to earn the follow-up question, not to empty the
magazine in one breath.

- **A** = the *shape* of problem you like solving. A shape, not a buzzword.
- **I** = the personal reason or outcome that matches: a skill, an experience, an impact you care about.

### Beat 2 — the follow-up, delivered only when asked

> "Working on **[product or team B]** is interesting because **[characteristic of B]**, and I **[personal reason J]**."

ONE product or team. ONE personal hook. Parallel grammar with Beat 1.

- **B** = a specific product, team, or surface. Not the whole company.
- **J** = a true, falsifiable personal hook: a skill, a project, an experience, a hobby. Never invented.

### Optional Beat 3 — held in reserve

A product opinion or proof point, used only if they push deeper. Include one only if it is
genuinely strong. Do not pad.

## Filling the slots — the three levers

A, I, B and J are the structure. These are where the substance comes from. A strong answer
lands at least one; the best land two.

### 1. Company research

Show you did homework, with something specific and checkable. Research reads as passion, and
passionate people make good hires.

> "What piqued my interest was a presentation your lead UI designer made about the different
> methods you use for data analysis, and how this helps you build a better product. I'm very
> quantitative, so I really want to work for such a data-driven company."

Sources worth mining before the interview: engineering blog, changelog, the founders' talks
and podcasts, the docs, recent launches, the JD's own vocabulary, and the product itself.
Specificity is the whole point. If the detail could apply to any company in the category,
it isn't research.

### 2. Relevant experience

Let the answer double as evidence you can do the job.

> "I'm really interested in testing tools. For my senior project in college, I built an
> automated way of detecting certain types of errors. I learned a ton about different types of
> website errors and ways to detect them. I was really intrigued by how much impact automated
> testing can do, if done well, but I also learned just how challenging it is to do well.
> I'm excited to get back into this space and leverage what I learned."

Note the shape: interest, the thing you built, what you learned, the honest hard part, and
why you want back in. The honest hard part is what makes it credible.

### 3. Passion

State plainly why the problem matters to you. Strongest at startups and at companies with a
mission that has a clear beneficiary.

> "I grew up fairly poor and attended pretty mediocre elementary schools. What helped me excel
> beyond my peers and win a scholarship to a top high school and college was seeking out extra
> resources. At that time, this meant public libraries, mentors, and a small number of websites.
> I think your company has the potential to bring these online resources to a much broader
> community of disadvantaged students. This was so meaningful to me growing up that I really
> want to be a part of that now."

Only use a story that is true. A borrowed origin story collapses on the first follow-up.

## Calibration

**Big companies** are less moved by research or by how much you want to work there. They
assume you do. Lead with fit for the **role or team**, and with experience that makes you good
at it. Company-level enthusiasm is the weakest lever here.

**Startups** care about passion for the space and about expertise in it. Use the product before
the interview and have an opinion about it, ideally one specific enough to be argued with.
That single move separates you from most of the pipeline.

## Do not say

- "It's a good stepping stone for my career." Reads as: leaving in 18 months.
- "It would look great on my resume." Same.
- "The financial upside." Reads as money-motivated, and as not understanding what a startup costs.
- Bare praise with nothing behind it: great culture, changing the world, everyone uses it.
- Anything critical of a current or former employer.

## Preparation

Come with **two or three reasons** written down before the interview, and practice them out
loud. If several interviewers in the loop ask the same question, give substantially the same
answer. Consistency is expected; a different reason for each interviewer looks improvised.

## Canonical worked example

**Beat 1 (A + I):** "I'm interested in working for AWS because I enjoy tackling problems at
a large scale **(A)**, and seeing a broad impact of my work for many customers **(I)**."

**Beat 2 (B + J):** "Working for EC2 is interesting as it has a close hardware-software
connection **(B characteristic)**, and I enjoy tinkering with arduino projects in my spare
time **(J)**."

## How to run this

1. Read `cv.md`, `article-digest.md`, `config/profile.yml`, `config/narrative.md`, and the
   evaluation report in `reports/` if one exists for this company.
2. Research the company for the specific detail: engineering blog, launches, docs, product.
   Use the `browser-automation` skill for anything bot-walled. Cite what you found.
3. Pick A, I, B, J. If the user named a team or product, that is B.
4. Decide which of the three levers each beat is carrying, and calibrate for company size.
5. Deliver **both beats**, labeled, so they can be used in sequence.
6. Follow with a table mapping the answer to the four slots:

   | Slot | Content | Lever |
   |---|---|---|
   | A | | research / experience / passion |
   | I | | |
   | B | | |
   | J | | |

7. Flag anything you could not verify from the user's own files. Ask, do not invent.
8. Run the draft through `avoid-ai-writing`: no em dashes, no "passionate about", no setup
   phrases, no clichés. It must sound spoken.
